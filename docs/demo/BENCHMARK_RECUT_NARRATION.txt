In February 2020, ACTT-1 registered a seven-point ordinal scale at day fifteen. The paper reports time to recovery. The registry says that outcome changed on April sixteenth, in version fourteen of twenty-five. Protocol Mirror found that from the live registry—and it cannot decide what the change means.

This is not a slideshow of invented data. The page opens on real ClinicalTrials.gov and PubMed records. Seven typed Web M C P tools let the agent read the trial, its publication, and the registration history as untrusted evidence.

The agent retrieves the exact original registry span and the paper's results, including stable source locators. It can inspect, cite, and stage a schema-bound proposal—but it has no tool that can accept or reject its own conclusion.

Here, it proposes an uncertain relationship. The evidence stays visible beside the rationale, so the reviewer can inspect the seven-point scale, the recovery result, and the dates instead of trusting a black-box summary.

The reviewer can reject with a reason. That feedback returns through the audit state, allowing the agent to revise and propose again. The two-way loop improves the work without transferring authority.

When the human accepts, the page changes from seven tools to eight. Only then can the agent export a reviewed receipt. The receipt identifies live sources, preserves the history-zero locator, and records the staged, rejected, revised, and accepted events.

We also tested twenty-four real trial-publication pairs. All forty-eight live source reads returned the requested record with nonempty evidence. Two small local models then failed in opposite directions—one overcalled changes while the other often missed them. These are run-specific grounding results, not clinical validation.

Protocol Mirror gives the agent the investigation, citation, revision, and packaging work. A person remains accountable for the consequential judgment. It is a research-transparency aid—not medical advice, a misconduct detector, or a clinical decision system.
